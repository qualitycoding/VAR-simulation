A verified adaptive-mesh solver for the 2D axisymmetric VAR model

A rebuild of a Vacuum Arc Remelting solver on body-fitted, solution-adaptive meshes. Its operators are verified to second order, its convection is exactly conservative, and its flow integrals converge at first order to a limit that does not depend on how the melt front is regularised.

Technical note  GHSC Research
Model  R. Polton, Numerical Grid Generation And Its Application In The Solution Of A Model Of The Vacuum-Arc Remelting (VAR) Process, University of Southampton, 1998, chapter 4
Status  operators and heat balance verified to second order; thermal and flow integrals converge at first order to regularisation-independent limits
Software  Python, NumPy and SciPy only

1Summary

This note reports a solver, its verification, and a convergence study whose flow results are extrapolated rather than computed directly.

The equations are those derived in chapter 4 of the thesis above: coupled stream function, vorticity, energy and magnetic induction equations for an axisymmetric ingot, non-dimensionalised as (4.39) to (4.42), transformed onto a body-fitted mesh, with a D'Arcy term representing the liquid-solid transition. The parameters are its Table 4.1.

What is established: the discrete operators are consistent and second-order accurate on curvilinear meshes; convection is discretised in the flux form of the thesis equations and is exactly conservative; the global heat balance converges to zero at second order, with or without flow; the magnetic induction equation is solved exactly with the thesis arc condition applied; and steady solutions exist along a continuation path spanning Grashof numbers from 2.9×106 to 2.0×108.

At the thesis verification parameters, on uniform meshes from 51×51 to 301×301 with all eighteen cases converged to 10-9 or better, the melt-front regularisation — a band a fixed number of cells wide — stops mattering as the mesh refines. The spread between one- and three-cell bands falls at first order, from 33% in kinetic energy at N = 101 to 10% at N = 301, and the three bands extrapolate separately to limits that agree within about 1.8%: kinetic energy about 253, circulation about 0.658, molten volume about 0.1389.

Two qualifications. The finest mesh is still 2 to 11% short of those limits in the flow integrals, so the extrapolated values are the result and carry an uncertainty of 1 to 2%. And an earlier version of this note reported different limits — kinetic energy about 235 — from a convection discretisation that omitted the vortex-stretching term of (4.41) and was not conservative. Those are withdrawn; section 8 explains how the omission arose and was found.

scope of claims

What a reader may and may not take from this note

Supported by mesh-convergence evidence: second-order operators; a heat balance that converges to zero at second order in every band tested; thermal integrals that converge at first order, with molten volume and thermal energy within 0.64% and 0.04% of their limits at N = 301; and flow integrals that converge at first order to a limit independent of the melt-front regularisation, estimated by extrapolation to within 1 to 2%. Not supported: any single-mesh flow value as a converged number. Everything is at the verification parameters; the demanding case has no convergence study.

2Formulation

The four governing equations are solved in the form given by the thesis, with the diffusion operators evaluated in conservative curvilinear form:

Cartesian part, conservative form lap(u) = (1/J) [ d_xi( (alpha u_xi - beta u_eta)/J ) + d_eta( (gamma u_eta - beta u_xi)/J ) ]

with the axisymmetric terms added through the metric expressions for uR and uZ. This is equivalent to the transformed equations (4.55) and (4.56) of the thesis but needs no explicit assembly of their first-derivative coefficients Δ1 and Γ1, which involve second derivatives of the coordinate fields and are awkward to difference stably.

Each equation is assembled as a sparse linear system and solved directly, so the residual of each discrete equation is zero to round-off after its own solve, and the only iteration is over the physical coupling. The stream function and vorticity are solved as one block: the vorticity sources the stream function through (4.39), and the stream function fixes the wall vorticity through (4.45) and (4.46), and that coupling is stiff enough that solving the two in sequence limit-cycles above Gr of about 5×106. Only the convective velocities are lagged.

Convergence is measured as the relative max-norm residual of each assembled system, never as the change between successive iterates. This distinction matters: an increment-based test applied to a heavily under-relaxed iteration reports convergence long before the solution arrives.

Deviations from the thesis, and why

D'Arcy sign. Equation (4.41) prints the D'Arcy group as +Cω on the left. Section 4.1 is explicit that the term exists to suppress motion below the melting temperature, that is, as a drag. With the drag sign the discrete operator is an M-matrix and the vorticity is driven towards zero in the solid; with the printed sign the diagonal changes sign once C exceeds the diffusive scale. The drag sign is used.

D'Arcy magnitude. C0 = 109 rather than the thesis 1010. At 1010 the residual stalls on the melting isotherm, where C jumps by about 109 between neighbouring nodes — which is the failure the thesis itself anticipates, quoting Hibbert, Markatos and Voller on C being so large as to cause rapid change in velocity and possible convergence failure. Where both converge, the two values give identical solutions to four significant figures.

Mushy-zone regularisation. The thesis uses a Heaviside step at the melting isotherm. Applied node-wise that step makes the fixed-point map discontinuous, since a node flipping between C = 0 and C = 109 between outer iterations moves the whole vorticity field. The step is instead ramped over a band a fixed number of computational cells wide, using the change in temperature across one cell as the ramp interval, with a C2 “smootherstep” profile across the band. Section 4 explains the choice of band, and section 3 why the profile must be smooth: an earlier clipped-linear ramp, kinked at both ends of the band, caused the iteration to limit-cycle on some meshes.

Convection in flux form. Both convective terms are discretised in the flux form in which the thesis writes them: Pr (1/R)[(RUθ)R + (RWθ)Z] for energy (4.42), and (Uω)R + (Wω)Z for vorticity (4.41). The volume flux through each control-volume face is the difference of the stream function between the face's two corners, with corner values taken as the mean of the four surrounding nodes, so the discrete velocity field is exactly divergence-free and energy is conserved to round-off. Face values are upwinded, with a van Leer-limited correction applied by deferred correction; measured on a manufactured solution this is about order 1.55 in the L2 norm and order 1 in the max norm, the limiter reverting to first order at extrema, with errors about thirty times smaller than plain upwinding. The vorticity form matters beyond conservation: expanded with the continuity equation it equals UωR + WωZ − Uω/R, and the last term, the vortex-stretching term, is essential to the axisymmetric vorticity equation. An earlier discretisation of the advective form UωR + WωZ omitted it.

This is a correction to an earlier version of this note. With the original advective convection the imbalance appeared to converge at order 2.36 over N = 51 to 101, then changed sign and tended to about −2×10-4 of the heat input on finer meshes. With the flow switched off it converged to zero at exactly order 2.00, which located the defect in the convective term; the flux form removes it. The earlier apparent order was pre-asymptotic and is withdrawn.

Front-dependent integrals converge at first order. Fitting v(h) = v0 + c hp over the six meshes, molten volume gives p = 1.13 (1.04 to 1.22 within twice the best misfit), thermal energy 1.12 (1.02 to 1.23) and the surface temperature integral 1.29. First-order fits have R2 of 0.996 to 0.999 and put N = 301 within 0.64%, 0.04% and 0.03% of the limits respectively. First order is what the regularisation would lead one to expect: the melt-front band is a fixed number of cells wide, so the error it introduces scales with h whatever the order of the operators. That explanation is consistent with the data but has not been tested separately.

Why the liquid-fraction profile had to change. (These experiments used the original advective convection.) With the earlier clipped-linear ramp, the Picard iteration did not converge at N = 76: it settled onto a limit cycle of period 450 sweeps, with residuals repeating exactly. It also failed at N = 91, and stalled near 10-6 at N = 101 for one- and three-cell bands. Damping, heavier pseudo-transient terms and Anderson acceleration did not help. Replacing the ramp with a C2 profile of the same support removed every one of these failures: all seven meshes above, and all nine cases of the band study in section 4, converge to 10-10 or better. The profile change moves the converged thermal integrals only slightly — molten volume by −0.30% at N = 51, shrinking to −0.10% at N = 101, as expected when two regularisations of the same step narrow with the mesh.

The mechanism is only partly established. At the smooth-profile fixed point at N = 76, the leading eigenvalues of the Picard map are a complex pair of modulus 0.907 — a damped oscillation with a period of about 333 sweeps. That fits the picture of the kinked ramp pushing such a mode outside the unit circle and producing the observed limit cycle. But it could not be confirmed: Newton–Krylov applied to the fixed-point equation with the linear ramp did not converge, even with a tight Krylov tolerance, so it remains undetermined whether the linear-ramp problem has a steady solution that Picard fails to hold, or none at N = 76. The linear-ramp results are retained in the repository for comparison.

A note on what is not in that table. Point extrema such as the maximum stream function, and the pool depth located by interpolating the melting isotherm, were used as convergence metrics in an earlier version of this work and produced fitted orders of 0.16 and −6 — that is, nonsense. Neither is better than first order and both are sensitive to where nodes happen to fall. They are reported elsewhere in this note for description but are not used to assess convergence.

4The mushy-zone regularisation, and how the flow converges

This section is the reason the note is framed as it is. It is reported at length because the failure mode is instructive and is easy to miss.

Regularising the Heaviside requires choosing a band over which C varies. The obvious choices — a fixed width in temperature, or a fixed physical distance across the isotherm — both leave a free parameter. The regularisation is not smoothing a discontinuity; it is setting the flow amplitude. Thermal quantities are almost untouched — 0.16% on the heat balance, 0.37% on thermal energy — which is consistent, since the energy equation does not see the D'Arcy term directly.

Replacing the fixed width with a band a fixed number of cells wide removes the free parameter in the continuum limit: the band narrows with the mesh and recovers the Heaviside as h tends to zero, while remaining a continuous function of the nodal temperatures. The relevant test is then whether the answer becomes band-independent under refinement. Two further signs that the asymptotic range has not been reached: at fixed band width the kinetic energy still climbs monotonically with N, and convergence failures reappear at the narrow end, where the stiffness that motivated the regularisation returns.

These spreads are within half a percentage point of those obtained with the earlier linear ramp, so the change of profile that cured the convergence failures does nothing by itself for the flow problem. What resolves it is refinement.

The difference is the vortex-stretching term; the earlier values solved a different equation.

With the thesis arc condition (4.32) applied, the magnetic induction equation solves exactly (residual 2.2×10-16) and gives a peak non-dimensional field of 0.3989. That ratio is invariant under the viscosity scaling used for the continuation, so it holds along the whole path. With the material parameters of Table 4.1 the Lorentz force does not drive this flow; the circulation is Marangoni-driven with a buoyancy contribution. This contradicts a claim made in an earlier draft of this work, which described a magnetic pinch opposing the surface flow. No such effect is present.

6Limitations

Flow values come from extrapolation, not from any one mesh. The flow integrals converge at first order to a regularisation-independent limit (section 4), but at N = 301 they are still 2 to 11% short of it. The limits are estimates uncertain at 1 to 2%.

Results before the flux-form convection solved a different equation. The continuation to Gr = 2.0×108, the electromagnetic force ratio, the D'Arcy suppression ratio, the liquid-fraction experiments and the stability analysis of the Picard map all used the original advective scheme, which omitted the vortex-stretching term. They are labelled where they appear and have not been recomputed.

The D'Arcy term does not arrest the solid. (Measured with the original advective convection.) Velocity in the solid region is suppressed by about a factor of 12 relative to the liquid, not the three orders of magnitude specified by Hibbert, Markatos and Voller. This appears structural rather than a matter of tuning: the term acts on the vorticity equation, so it drives the solid irrotational, after which the stream function satisfies a homogeneous Poisson equation there and decays away from the pool without vanishing. The thesis anticipates the qualitative point in section 4.1, observing that non-zero velocity in the solid is unrealistic but acceptable given the advantage of a single equation across both regions. The quantitative size of the gap between the streamfunction-vorticity and primitive-variable formulations is reported here but has not been analysed, and the structural explanation above, while consistent with the evidence, has not been proved.

Continuation stops at Gr = 2.0×108. Beyond that the Picard iteration with lagged convection loses stability and no steady solution was obtained. Whether this is loss of a stable steady laminar branch or a limitation of the iteration has not been determined. A Newton method on the fully coupled system, or time-accurate integration, would settle it. No claim is made either way, and in particular the full unscaled Table 4.1 parameters (Gr = 2.9×1010) remain out of reach here.

Convergence depends on a smooth liquid-fraction profile, and the margin is small. A clipped-linear ramp made the iteration limit-cycle or stall on several meshes; the C2 profile now used removes every failure found. But at the one fixed point examined, the Picard map has spectral radius 0.907, so the iteration is stable without much to spare, and the more demanding cases may yet fail. Why the linear ramp fails is only partly established (section 3).

Front-dependent quantities converge at first order. The melt-front band is a fixed number of cells wide, so its error scales with h whatever the order of the operators. Molten volume, thermal energy, the surface temperature integral and all flow integrals converge at first order or a little below. Higher order would need a different treatment of the front.

All convergence evidence is at the verification case. The studies above were run at the section 4.5.1 parameters (Gr = 2.9×106). At Gr = 2.0×108 no refinement study was possible within the compute available, and the continuation results there are descriptive.

The accuracy gain from adaptation is suggested, not demonstrated. The adaptation clusters correctly and does not fold. In the one case compared, the adapted 101×101 mesh of Figure 1 gives a kinetic energy of 228.7, against 198.9 on the uniform 101×101 mesh and 226.6 on a uniform 201×201 mesh, which has four times as many nodes. The same pattern held under the original convection scheme. A plausible reason is that the melt-front band, being a fixed number of cells wide, is physically narrower where the adapted mesh clusters at the front. One case is not a demonstration; that would need an adapted refinement sequence extrapolated to the same limit.

7Software

Pure Python on NumPy and SciPy. Two components are worth noting for anyone reimplementing.

The grid generator solves the Thompson-Thames-Mastin elliptic system with control functions derived from a monitor function blending normalised vorticity magnitude and normalised temperature-gradient magnitude. It is not the Brackbill-Saltzman method, despite an earlier version of this software being named for it: Brackbill-Saltzman minimises a weighted sum of smoothness, orthogonality and volume integrals, whose Euler-Lagrange equations (thesis 3.14 and 3.21) contain none of the terms solved here. The forcing is scaled by the absolute Jacobian rather than its square, an empirical rescaling that compensates for normalising the computational spacings to unity, and which means the equation solved is not the stationarity condition of any stated functional.

Point relaxation on the grid equation does not converge usefully at practical resolutions. At 101×101 the residual falls like the reciprocal of the sweep count: 12,800 point Gauss-Seidel sweeps took it only from 2.7×10-1 to 6.4×10-2. Picard iteration with the metric coefficients frozen and a direct sparse solve reaches 2.1×10-7 in 27 outer iterations and about two seconds. Any implementation that runs a fixed, modest number of point sweeps is returning a partially relaxed mesh rather than a solution of its grid equation.

8Errors made during this work

None was caught by a residual. All were caught by cross-checking, and all are easy to repeat.

A fix that makes the residual behave is not thereby correct. Widening the mushy band to cure a residual stall produced a cleanly converged solution on a spurious branch, with the circulation reduced to a third and the pool depth cut by two thirds. It converged beautifully. It was wrong, and only a comparison against a stiffer, harder-to-converge run revealed it.

Convergence metrics have to be chosen before they are trusted. The first refinement study of this work used point extrema and an interpolated isotherm position and returned fitted orders of 0.16 and −6. The discretisation was not at fault; the functionals were, compounded by a regularisation whose physical width varied with the mesh. Reporting those orders as evidence of anything would have been an error of exactly the kind this rebuild was undertaken to correct.

A term was dropped from the vorticity equation, and a global check found it before a local one did. The original convection discretised the advective form UωR + WωZ, which is equivalent to the thesis form (Uω)R + (Wω)Z only in a planar flow. In axisymmetric flow the two differ by the vortex-stretching term Uω/R, and the advective form omits it. Every earlier flow result in this work, including a set of carefully extrapolated limits published in a previous version of this note, solved that incomplete equation. Nothing in the residuals, the mesh-convergence study or the band-independence study could detect it: all of them test whether the discrete equations are solved and converge, not whether they are the right equations. What exposed it was a physical conservation check — the heat balance tending to a non-zero limit — which led to rewriting the convection in the thesis's own flux form, and so to the missing term. The lesson is the one this rebuild began with: agreement between refinements verifies a discretisation, not a model.

A bookkeeping error left a coefficient of about 1016 in the matrix. At the corner where the axis meets the baseplate, the baseplate's wall-vorticity condition couples ω to ψ with coefficient −2/(RΔz2), and R = 0 there. The axis condition overrides that row, but the coupling was stored separately and survived the override. It multiplied a stream-function value that is zero only to round-off. It is fixed; its effect on earlier results was not measured separately.

9Outstanding work

  • Recompute the results listed in section 6 that predate the flux-form convection: the continuation to Gr = 2.0×108, the force and suppression ratios, and the stability of the Picard map.
  • Determine whether the Gr = 2.0×108 ceiling is a bifurcation or an iteration failure.
  • Quantify, rather than assert, the irrotational leakage of the D'Arcy formulation, ideally against a primitive-variable solution of the same problem.
  • Establish whether the linear-ramp problem has a steady solution at N = 76 that Picard fails to hold. Newton–Krylov did not converge; a better-preconditioned Newton or continuation in the profile smoothness would settle it.
  • Narrow the uncertainty on the flow limits, which rests on four meshes: N = 401 fits in 3 GB, and N = 601 needs a larger machine or an iterative solver in place of sparse LU.
  • Repeat the mesh-convergence study at the demanding parameters once the above is settled.
